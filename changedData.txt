This page is also available as\nMarkdown\n.\nGossip (read) priority\nThere are 3 independent Dutch auctions synced to the same 3 minute schedule. The auction indices are optionally interpreted by nodes as an ordering for their peers when sending data. The priority ordering affects both split client blocks and normal client blocks that include responses. The foundation non-validator will opt into respecting the gossip priority auction ordering.\nThe independent slot auctions are not additive, so an IP bidding multiple slots will be prioritized according to its lowest slot. For the auction to affect latency, the onchain IP must exactly match the IP seen by the peer sending data. Each auction's results only affect the duration of the following auction. For nodes that are connected via a chain of nodes to the validating network, note that each network hop may or may not respect the priority auction depending on how the parent node is configured.\nThe lower auction indices are strictly prioritized over higher indices. The prioritization applies to all data in the mempool transactions stream equally. The ordering should be relatively consistent in expectation but is subject to variance from the p2p consensus network. Gossip priority applies only to reading data. For prioritization of sending data, see the section below on order priority. Nodes respecting gossip priority will use the previous auction winners relative to that node's current execution state. Similar to token, spot, and perp deployment auctions, fees come out of spot balance are burned.\nThe previous auction winners (i.e., current gossip priority ordering) and current auction statuses can be queried using the info request\n{ \"type\": \"gossipPriorityAuctionStatus\"}\n.\nGossip priority reduces the need for market makers to hyper-optimize their infrastructure to remain competitive. To participate in the auction, users send action\n{\"type\": \"gossipPriorityBid\", \"slotId\": 0, \"ip\": \"1.2.3.4\", \"maxGas\": 100000000}\nwhere gas is wei (i.e. units of\n0.00000001\nHYPE) of HYPE charged from spot balance. Each auction resets to 10 times the previous winning price of that slot, and have a minimum price of\n0.1 HYPE\n. Any address may bid on behalf of any IP address.\nThe current empirical effect of gossip priority on mainnet is approximately 20 ms reduction in latency per auction slot.\nOrder (write) priority\nOrder priority reduces the need for market makers to hyper-optimize their order entry and connectivity, and instead focus on the strategy itself. It further protects makers and allows responsiveness to price moves driven by other venues.\nPriority grouping is only supported for order actions where both\nevery order is on a non-outcome asset\nevery order is IOC, or every order is a non-reduce-only ALO\nOrders can be sent with grouping of the form\n{\"p\": 12345}\nwhere the rate is interpreted as a fraction\np / 100000000.0\n. The rate is charged from undelegated staking balance as a fraction of the filled notional in the case of IOC, and resting notional in the case of ALO. The priority gas charged is converted to HYPE using the spot mark price.\nIOC priority\nThe priority rate has a linear effect on end-to-end latency from 0-8bps, i.e.\np = 80000\n. The current empirical effect of order priority fees on mainnet is approximately 45 ms reduction in end-to-end latency per 1 bp of priority fee paid. Even with order priority, all cancels are prioritized before all immediately executable orders. However, higher priority orders are prioritized before lower priority orders as a linear function of priority rate.\nThe max priority rate is 100%. Any priority between 8bps and 100% is treated with identical time preference in the mempool. However, IOC orders with at least 8bps priority fee that would be executed by the block proposer in each 70ms unix time bucket (usually corresponding to 1 block) are sorted in decreasing order of priority fee. In other words, the 0-8bps range effects temporal prioritization, and 8bps-100% range breaks ties among orders in this range received at a similar time. As a concrete example, if the unix milliseconds boundary is\n(T, T + 70ms)\n, the orders with\n(mempool_receive_time, priority)\nequal to\n(T, 8bps)\n,\n(T + 30ms, 9bps)\n,\n(T + 69ms, 10bps)\nwould be sorted in reverse order of receive time.\nAlthough blocks are discrete, order prioritization is continuous with respect to order write priority fee. The mempool transactions are effectively sorted by\neffective_time = arrival_time + f(action, priority_fee)\nwhere\nf\nis a strictly decreasing function of priority fee, and zero for prioritized actions such as cancels. For a given action type, boundaries between blocks are not relevant for relative prioritization, as the transactions within a block are still sorted according to\neffective_time\n.\nOrder priority applies only to sending data. For prioritization of reading data, see the previous section on gossip priority. Similar to gossip priority auctions, order priority fees are burned.\nIOC priority fee paid can be read from the\nuser_fills\nfile written by the nodes as field\npriorityGas\n.\nALO priority\nOn the timescale of\nT = 400ms\n, ALO orders are sorted by priority. More precisely, the tail of the queue at each level consisting of orders placed within the past\nT\nwill be sorted in decreasing order of priority rate.\nAs a consequence, any slot in the queue is eligible for priority fee bidding for a window of duration\nT\n.  Each new order compares against the tail of orders less than\nT\nold at placement time. In other words, the\nT\nwindow is continuous, not bucketed. ALO priority fees are deducted at time of placing the order regardless of whether the order fills.\nUnlike for IOC orders, ALO priority fees do not effect mempool prioritization. ALO orders are processed FIFO as transactions always, with similar end-to-end latency as cancels. The priority fee implementation affects place in queue across transactions. In other words, ALO orders are prioritized similarly in the mempool regardless of their priority, but queue position can change on a 400ms time scale after orders transactions are executed on the L1.\nA concrete example: a new level\nL\nopens and is empty at time\nt\n. The first order\nA\nat level\nL\nis applied onchain (as above, this means executed on the L1) at time\nt' > t\nwith zero priority fee. The next order\nB\nis applied onchain at time\nt' + T - 1ms\nwith priority\np > 0\n. Assuming\nA\nand\nB\nare the only orders at level\nL\n, the ordering at time\nt' + T\nwill have\nB\nin front of\nA\n. A third order\nC\nis applied at time\nt' + T + 1ms\nwith priority\np' > p\n. The ordering at time\nt' + T + 1ms\nis\nB > A > C\n.
